Quid Pro Quo Contributions: The $75 Disclosure Rule Explained

Fundraising dinners, galas, concerts and events where donors get something back come with one extra IRS rule. Here's what the disclosure must say, when to give it, the exceptions, and sample wording, with links to the IRS sources.

The short answer

When a donor pays more than $75 and the payment is partly a gift and partly for something your organization provides (a dinner, a ticket, merchandise), your organization must give the donor a written disclosure. It tells the donor that only the part of the payment above the value of what they received is deductible, and it gives a good-faith estimate of that value. You can give it when you ask for the money or when you receive it. Unlike the $250 acknowledgment rule, this one is the charity's job, and there is a penalty for skipping it.

What counts as a quid pro quo contribution

The IRS describes a quid pro quo contribution as "a payment a donor makes to a charity partly as a contribution and partly for goods or services." Publication 1771 says goods or services include cash, property, services, benefits, or privileges.

IRS example: a donor gives a charity $100 and receives a concert ticket with a fair market value of $40. The charitable contribution part of the payment is $60. Because the donor's payment is more than $75, the charity must provide a disclosure statement, even though the deductible part ($60) is not more than $75.

So the $75 test looks at the whole payment, not just the deductible part. Common situations for churches and small nonprofits include ticketed dinners, galas, concerts, and gifts that come with a thank-you item worth more than a token amount.

Charity auctions can raise their own questions (for example, how a winning bid compares with the item's value). This page sticks to what the IRS sources below say about quid pro quo disclosures. Ask a tax professional about auction specifics.

What the disclosure must say: the two required elements

According to IRS Publication 1771 and the IRS quid pro quo page, the written disclosure statement must:

  1. Inform the donor that the amount of the contribution that is deductible for federal income tax purposes is limited to the excess of any money (and the value of any property other than money) contributed by the donor over the fair market value of the goods or services provided by the charity, and
  2. Provide a good-faith estimate of the fair market value of the goods or services the donor received.

Publication 1771 explains why: donors can only deduct the amount by which their contribution exceeds the value of what they received, so they need to know that value.

When and how to give it

  • Timing: furnish the statement in connection with either the solicitation or the receipt of the quid pro quo contribution. If you give it with a particular solicitation (an invitation or ticket order form, for example), the IRS says you don't need to give another statement when you actually receive the contribution.
  • In writing: the statement must be in writing.
  • Noticeable: it must be made in a way that is likely to come to the donor's attention. Publication 1771 warns that a disclosure in small print within a larger document might not meet this requirement.

Good-faith estimates of fair market value

The IRS says an organization may use any reasonable method to estimate the fair market value of what it provided, as long as it applies the method in good faith. For goods or services that generally aren't commercially available, you may use the value of similar or comparable goods or services, even if they lack the unique qualities of what you're valuing. The IRS gives three examples:

PaymentWhat the donor receivedGood-faith estimate (per IRS)
First $500 paymentOne-hour tennis lesson with a pro who charges $100 commercially$100
$50,000Private event in a museum room; a comparable hotel ballroom rents for $2,500$2,500
$1,000Evening museum tour by a well-known artist who doesn't give tours commercially; tours are normally free$0

For a fundraising dinner, a practical approach is to ask what a comparable meal would cost at a local restaurant or caterer, and keep a note of how you arrived at the number. (That's our suggestion, not IRS wording.)

When no disclosure is required

The IRS lists these situations where a written disclosure statement isn't required:

  • Token items (insubstantial value). Goods or services of insubstantial value, as described in Revenue Procedures 90-12 and 92-49. Publication 1771 explains that this applies when the payment is made in the context of a fundraising campaign, the charity tells the donor how much of the payment is deductible, and either (1) the value of what the donor receives doesn't exceed the lesser of 2 percent of the payment or a set dollar cap, or (2) the payment is at least a set minimum, the only items provided bear the organization's name or logo (calendars, mugs, posters), and their total cost is within the "low-cost articles" limit. Low-cost articles sent free to people who didn't order them, like mailing labels or greeting cards, also count as insubstantial.
  • No donative element. For example, a typical purchase from a museum gift shop.
  • Only intangible religious benefits. Provided by an organization organized exclusively for religious purposes and of a type not generally sold in commercial transactions outside a donative context, such as admission to a religious ceremony that has no admission charge. Tuition for education leading to a recognized degree, travel services, and consumer goods are not intangible religious benefits.
  • Low-cost membership benefits. The donor pays $75 or less per year and receives only annual membership benefits: rights or privileges they can use often during the membership period (such as free or discounted admission or parking, or preferred access to goods or services, but not the right to buy college athletic tickets), or admission to members-only events whose per-person cost is within the low-cost-article limits.

The token-exception dollar figures

Publication 1771 (Rev. 11-2023) prints the figures for 2023 and notes they are adjusted for inflation. The IRS's annual inflation-adjustment revenue procedure for 2026 (Rev. Proc. 2025-32, section 4.33) updates the same guidelines:

Guideline2023 (Pub. 1771)Taxable years beginning in 2026 (Rev. Proc. 2025-32)

IRS example (2023 figures): a charity gives a coffee mug with its logo that costs the organization $12.50 or less to a donor who contributes $62.50 or more. The organization may state that no goods or services were provided, and the contribution is fully deductible.

These figures change every year. Check the current revenue procedure at irs.gov before relying on them.

The penalty for not disclosing

The IRS imposes a penalty on a charity that doesn't make the required disclosure for a quid pro quo contribution of more than $75: $10 per contribution, not to exceed $5,000 per fundraising event or mailing. The charity can avoid the penalty if it can show the failure was due to reasonable cause.

A payment can trigger both rules. A $300 gala ticket, for example, is over $75 and $250 or more. Many organizations use one letter or receipt that includes every item both rules call for. Worked example

The IRS example: $100 payment, concert ticket worth $40. Deductible amount: no more than $100 − $40 = $60. Disclosure required, because the payment is over $75.

An illustrative example (our numbers, not the IRS's): your church sells $150 tickets to a spring fundraising dinner. You estimate in good faith that a comparable catered dinner costs about $55 per person.

ItemAmount
Ticket price paid$150
Good-faith estimate of the dinner's value$55
Amount that may be deductible (at most)$150 − $55 = $95

The $150 payment is over $75, so the disclosure is required. Put it on the invitation or ticket order form, or on the receipt you send afterward. A donor who buys two tickets for $300 in one payment has made a single payment of $250 or more, so they would also need a written acknowledgment to claim a deduction.

Sample disclosure wording

Sample only. This is our example wording built from the two elements the IRS lists. It is not IRS text, not an IRS form, and not legal advice. Replace the bracketed parts with your own details.

On an invitation or ticket form (at solicitation):

"Tickets are $150 per person. Our good-faith estimate of the fair market value of the dinner is $55 per person. The amount of your payment that is deductible for federal income tax purposes is limited to the excess of the money (and the value of any property other than money) you contribute over the value of the goods or services we provide."

On a receipt (at receipt):

"Thank you for your payment of $150 that [organization name] received on [date] for the [event name]. In exchange, we provided a dinner with a good-faith estimated fair market value of $55. The amount of your contribution that is deductible for federal income tax purposes is limited to the excess of the money (and the value of any property other than money) you contributed over the value of the goods or services we provided."

Make the wording easy to see, not tucked into fine print.

Frequently asked questions

What is a quid pro quo contribution?

The IRS describes it as a payment a donor makes to a charity partly as a contribution and partly for goods or services, such as a $100 payment for a concert ticket valued at $40.

Does the $75 rule look at the whole payment or only the deductible part?

The whole payment. In the IRS example, a donor pays $100 for a $40 ticket. Only $60 is deductible, but a disclosure is still required because the payment itself is more than $75.

When do we have to give the disclosure?

In connection with either the solicitation or the receipt of the payment. The IRS says that if you give the disclosure with a particular solicitation, you don't have to give another one when you receive the payment.

Can the disclosure be in the fine print on a ticket?

It must be in writing and made in a way that is likely to come to the donor's attention. IRS Publication 1771 says a disclosure in small print within a larger document might not meet this requirement.

What is the penalty for not disclosing?

The IRS says the penalty is $10 per contribution, not to exceed $5,000 per fundraising event or mailing. The charity can avoid it if it shows the failure was due to reasonable cause.

Do we need a disclosure if we only give a small logo item?

Not if the item meets the IRS token exception for goods or services of insubstantial value. The limits are adjusted for inflation each year, so check the current figures.

Does a church need a disclosure for worship services?

No disclosure is required when only an intangible religious benefit is provided, such as admission to a religious ceremony, by an organization organized exclusively for religious purposes.
