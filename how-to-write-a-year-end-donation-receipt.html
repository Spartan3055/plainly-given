How to Write a Year-End Donation Receipt or Church Contribution Statement (IRS Requirements)

A plain-English walkthrough of what the IRS says a donor acknowledgment must contain, when donors need it, and wording you can use, with links to the IRS sources.

The short answer

A year-end donation receipt (often called a contribution statement or giving statement) is a written acknowledgment from your organization to a donor. For any single gift of $250 or more, the donor needs one to claim a federal tax deduction. It has to name your organization, show the amount of each cash gift, describe (not value) any donated property, and say whether you gave the donor anything in return. There's no official IRS form, so a letter or computer-generated statement works.

When a written acknowledgment is required

According to IRS Publication 1771, a donor can't claim a federal income tax deduction for any single contribution of $250 or more unless they get a contemporaneous written acknowledgment from the charity. This applies to cash gifts and to non-cash gifts valued at $250 or more.

  • Each gift counts separately. If someone gives $25 every week, those gifts aren't added together for the $250 test (IRS Publication 526).
  • Payroll deductions: each paycheck deduction is treated as a separate contribution.
  • The donor carries the responsibility. Your organization won't be penalized for not sending one, but the donor loses the deduction without it. That's why nearly every church and nonprofit sends statements anyway.
  • Donors still need a bank record or written communication for every cash gift, even small ones. A complete annual statement covers that too.

The 6 things it must include

IRS Publication 1771 says the charity's written statement should contain:

  1. The name of the organization.
  2. The amount of any monetary contribution.
  3. A description (but not the fair market value) of any contribution of property.
  4. A statement that no goods or services were provided by the organization in return for the contribution, if that was the case.
  5. If goods or services were provided, a description and good-faith estimate of their fair market value.
  6. If the organization provided only intangible religious benefits, a statement saying so.

You don't need the donor's Social Security number. Most organizations also include the donor's name and address, the date of each gift, your address, and a signature. None of those are on the IRS list, but donors and their tax preparers expect them.

The deadline: what "contemporaneous" means

The donor must receive the acknowledgment on or before the earlier of (a) the date they file their tax return for the year of the gift, or (b) the return's due date, including extensions. The IRS notes that charities typically send acknowledgments no later than January 31 of the following year. Aim for the third or fourth week of January so early filers aren't left waiting.

Paper and electronic delivery are both fine. Publication 1771 specifically allows sending it by email to the donor.

Annual statements: list every gift and its date

You may send a separate receipt for each gift, or one annual summary covering all of a donor's gifts. If you use one statement for several gifts of $250 or more, IRS Publication 526 says it should list each contribution and the date of each contribution and show the total. A statement that only says "Total giving in 2026: $3,600" is weaker than one that lists every date and amount.

Non-cash (in-kind) gifts

For donated property (clothing, furniture, food, equipment), describe the items but don't state a value. Example: "6 bags of gently used adult clothing and 2 winter coats." Determining the value is the donor's job. Additional rules can apply:

  • If a donor deducts more than $500 for non-cash gifts, they file Form 8283.
  • For an item (or group of similar items) over $5,000, the donor generally needs a qualified appraisal, and your organization may be asked to sign Section B of Form 8283.
  • Donated vehicles, boats and airplanes claimed at more than $500 have their own rules (Form 1098-C).

Goods or services, and the $75 quid pro quo rule

If the donor got something in return, such as a dinner, a concert ticket, or merchandise, the deductible amount is generally limited to what they gave minus the fair market value of what they received. When a payment is more than $75 and partly a gift and partly for goods or services, the organization must give a written disclosure that:

  • tells the donor the deductible amount is limited to the excess of what they contributed over the value of the goods or services, and
  • provides a good-faith estimate of the value of those goods or services.

IRS example: a donor gives $100 and receives a concert ticket worth $40. The deductible amount can't exceed $60. Because the payment is over $75, the disclosure is required, even though the deductible part is under $75. The penalty for not providing it is $10 per contribution, up to $5,000 per fundraising event or mailing (IRS Publication 1771).

There are exceptions for token items of insubstantial value (for example, a mug or calendar with your logo, within IRS low-cost limits that are adjusted each year) and certain low-cost membership benefits. Check the current figures in IRS Publication 1771 before relying on them.

Intangible religious benefits (churches)

If a religious organization provides only "intangible religious benefits," such as admission to a worship service or a de minimis item like communion wine, the acknowledgment doesn't need to describe or value them. It can simply say that only intangible religious benefits were provided. Things like tuition for a recognized degree, travel, or consumer goods don't count as intangible religious benefits.

Sample wording you can use

These examples come from IRS Publication 1771 (organization names in parentheses):

"Thank you for your cash contribution of $300 that (organization's name) received on December 12, 2015. No goods or services were provided in exchange for your contribution."
"Thank you for your cash contribution of $350 that (organization's name) received on May 6, 2015. In exchange for your contribution, we gave you a cookbook with an estimated fair market value of $60."
"Thank you for your contribution of a used oak baby crib and matching dresser that (organization's name) received on March 15, 2015. No goods or services were provided in exchange for your contribution."

For a church annual statement, a common goods-or-services line is:

"No goods or services were provided in exchange for these contributions other than intangible religious benefits."

And for event or quid pro quo payments:

"In exchange for your payment of $100, we provided a dinner with a good-faith estimated fair market value of $40. The amount of your contribution that is deductible for federal income tax purposes is limited to the excess of the amount of money (and the value of any property other than money) you contributed over the value of goods or services provided."

Special cases to watch

  • Pledges: acknowledge gifts actually received, not pledges.
  • Volunteers' out-of-pocket expenses: for unreimbursed expenses of $250 or more, the volunteer needs an acknowledgment describing the services they provided and whether you gave anything in return.
  • Gifts to a specific program or fund: you can name the fund, e.g. "made in the name of its Special Relief Fund program" (an IRS example).
  • Gifts made in late December: check-date and mailbox rules can matter. Record the date you actually received the gift and ask your tax adviser about edge cases.
  • Gifts to individuals (for example, a specific family) generally aren't deductible charitable contributions. Don't issue a receipt implying they are.

Year-end statement checklist

  • Reconcile your gift records with bank deposits for the full calendar year
  • Confirm each donor's name (or household) and mailing/email address
  • Separate cash gifts from non-cash gifts; describe property without values
  • Flag any gifts where the donor received goods or services (events, dinners, merchandise) and note a good-faith value
  • Choose the right goods-or-services statement for each donor
  • List each gift with its date and show the total
  • Have an authorized person review and sign
  • Send by January 31; keep a copy for your records

Frequently asked questions

Is there an official IRS form for a donation receipt?

No. The IRS says there are no IRS forms for the contemporaneous written acknowledgment. Letters, postcards, emails or computer-generated statements are acceptable if they contain the required information.

Do churches have to send contribution statements?

An organization that doesn't acknowledge a contribution incurs no penalty, but without a written acknowledgment the donor can't deduct any single gift of $250 or more. Charities are expected to help donors by providing one, and most churches send annual statements by January 31.

Do I need to put the donor's Social Security number on the receipt?

No. IRS Publication 1771 says it isn't necessary to include the donor's Social Security number or tax identification number.

Can I email donation receipts?

Yes. The IRS says an organization can provide the acknowledgment on paper or electronically, such as via email addressed to the donor.

Should I put a dollar value on donated items?

No. For non-cash contributions, the acknowledgment should include a description, but not the value, of the property. Valuing donated property is the donor's job.

Do small weekly gifts need a receipt?

Separate contributions under $250 aren't combined for the written-acknowledgment rule. Donors still need a bank record or written communication for every cash gift, so many churches include every gift on the annual statement.

What is the $75 quid pro quo rule?

If a donor pays more than $75 and receives goods or services in return (like a dinner or ticket), the organization must give a written disclosure stating that only the amount above the value of what they received is deductible, along with a good-faith estimate of that value.
