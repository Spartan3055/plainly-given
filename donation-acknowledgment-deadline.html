Donation Acknowledgment Deadline: When Is the Written Acknowledgment Due?

The IRS calls it a "contemporaneous written acknowledgment." Here's what that deadline actually is, how the $250 threshold works, what the acknowledgment must say, and what happens if it arrives late, with links to the IRS sources.

The short answer

For any single contribution of $250 or more, the donor needs a written acknowledgment from your organization, and they must have it in hand by the earlier of (1) the date they file their federal income tax return for the year of the gift, or (2) that return's due date, including extensions. That's the whole legal deadline. Federal law doesn't name a calendar date. January 31 is a common practice that the IRS mentions, not a statutory deadline. If the acknowledgment arrives too late, your organization pays no penalty, but the donor can't deduct that gift.

The legal deadline: the "earlier of" rule

Internal Revenue Code section 170(f)(8)(A) says no deduction is allowed for a contribution of $250 or more unless the donor substantiates it with a contemporaneous written acknowledgment from the charity. Section 170(f)(8)(C) then defines "contemporaneous": the acknowledgment counts if the taxpayer obtains it on or before the earlier of:

  1. the date the taxpayer files a return for the taxable year in which the contribution was made, or
  2. the due date (including extensions) for filing that return.

Treasury Regulation section 1.170A-13(f)(3) uses the same test and refers to the taxpayer's original return. Two things to notice:

  • It's tied to the donor, not to you. Every donor's deadline can differ, because it depends on when that donor files.
  • "Obtains" means the donor has it. A statement you mail the day after a donor files is too late for that donor's gifts of $250 or more.

Illustrative timelines (our examples, not IRS examples)

For calendar-year individual filers, the IRS says a filing extension gives them until October 15 to file. So, for gifts made in 2026:

The early filer is the one to plan for. You can't know who files in early February, which is why sending statements early in the year makes sense.

Why churches send statements by January 31 (practice, not law)

IRS Publication 1771 says: "Charities typically send written acknowledgments to donors no later than January 31 of the year following the donation." Publication 526 says the same about organizations in general. That's a description of common practice. Neither the Code section nor the regulation sets January 31 as a deadline.

It's still a sensible target. January 31 is early enough that most donors will have their statement before they file. Some treasurers aim for the third or fourth week of January to be safe. (That's our suggestion, not an IRS rule.)

The $250 threshold and the separate-contributions rule

The written-acknowledgment rule applies to any single contribution of $250 or more, cash or property. The regulation and IRS publications add some details:

  • Separate gifts aren't combined. Treasury Reg. 1.170A-13(f)(1) says separate contributions of less than $250 aren't subject to the rule, even if a donor's total for the year is $250 or more. Publication 526's example: if you give your church $25 each week, each payment is a separate contribution. Publication 1771 gives weekly church offerings under $250 as an example.
  • It's the contribution amount that counts. If a payment is partly for goods or services, the contribution is the part of the payment above their value. The regulation says the rule doesn't apply to a payment of $250 or more if the amount contributed is less than $250.
  • Payroll deductions: each deduction from each paycheck is a separate contribution (see below).

Even when a gift is under $250, it can still trigger the separate $75 quid pro quo disclosure rule if the donor paid more than $75 and got something back.

What the acknowledgment must contain

IRS Publication 1771 and the IRS written-acknowledgments page list what the charity's statement should include:

  1. the name of the organization;
  2. the amount of any cash (monetary) contribution;
  3. a description, but not the value, of any non-cash contribution;
  4. a statement that no goods or services were provided in return, if that's the case;
  5. if goods or services were provided, a description and good-faith estimate of their value; and
  6. if the only benefits were intangible religious benefits, a statement saying so.

Publication 1771 adds that the acknowledgment doesn't need the donor's Social Security number or tax ID number. There is no IRS form for it. Letters, postcards, emails and computer-generated statements with this information are acceptable. Publication 526 also notes that if the acknowledgment doesn't show the date of the contribution, the donor also needs a bank record or receipt that does, so it's worth including dates.

One annual statement or one receipt per gift

Publication 1771 says you may give a separate acknowledgment for each single contribution of $250 or more, or one acknowledgment, such as an annual summary, that covers several. If you use one statement for several gifts of $250 or more, Publication 526 says it should list each contribution and the date of each contribution and show the total.

Paper or electronic delivery both work. Publication 1771 specifically mentions email addressed to the donor. Donors don't attach the acknowledgment to their return. They keep it with their records.

Payroll-deduction gifts

Treasury Reg. 1.170A-13(f)(11) and Publication 1771 allow a different kind of substantiation for gifts withheld from wages. The donor can use both:

  • a pay stub, Form W-2, or other employer-furnished document showing the amount withheld and paid to the charity, and
  • a pledge card or other document prepared by or at the direction of the charity that states the organization doesn't provide goods or services in return for contributions made by payroll deduction.

Each payroll deduction is treated as a separate contribution for the $250 threshold, so the special rule matters when $250 or more is withheld from a single paycheck. Publication 526 says one pledge card can cover all payroll-deduction gifts if it has the required information. The same "earlier of filing or due date" timing applies. If the donor's documents don't show the date of the contribution, Publication 526 says the donor needs another document that does.

What happens if the acknowledgment is late

  • For the charity: Publication 1771 says "An organization that does not acknowledge a contribution incurs no penalty." (This is different from the $75 quid pro quo disclosure, which does carry a penalty.)
  • For the donor: without a contemporaneous written acknowledgment, the donor can't claim the deduction for a single gift of $250 or more. An acknowledgment the donor gets after the earlier of their filing date or the due date doesn't meet the definition of "contemporaneous."

Publication 1771 also says that, although getting the acknowledgment is the donor's responsibility, a charitable organization must assist donors by providing one. If a donor asks for a statement after you've sent yours, or says theirs never arrived, send it promptly. Whether a late statement still helps a particular donor is a question for their tax professional, not for the church office.

Gifts under $250 still need a record

The $250 rule isn't the only one. Publication 1771 says a donor can't deduct any monetary contribution unless they keep a bank record (such as a canceled check or bank or credit card statement) or a written communication from the charity showing the organization's name, the date and the amount. A charity's written communication counts only if the donor gets it by the same "earlier of" date. That's one reason many churches list every gift on the annual statement, not just the large ones.

A January checklist for treasurers

Our practical suggestions, based on the rules above (not IRS wording):

  • Close out the year's gift records in early January and reconcile them with deposits.
  • For each donor, list each gift with its date and amount, and show the total for the year.
  • Describe non-cash gifts without putting a value on them.
  • Include the right goods-or-services statement: "no goods or services," a description and good-faith estimate, or intangible religious benefits.
  • Send by email or mail by January 31, and earlier if you can.
  • Keep a copy and note the date each statement was sent.
  • Answer late requests quickly. Some donors file in early February.

FAQ

When is the deadline for a donation acknowledgment?

Under IRC section 170(f)(8)(C), the donor must obtain the written acknowledgment on or before the earlier of the date they file their return for the year of the gift or the return's due date, including extensions. Federal law doesn't set a specific calendar date for charities.

Is January 31 a legal deadline for church contribution statements?

No federal law sets January 31. IRS Publications 1771 and 526 say charities typically send written acknowledgments no later than January 31 of the year after the gift. It is a common practice that helps donors who file early.

What happens if our church sends the acknowledgment late?

IRS Publication 1771 says an organization that doesn't acknowledge a contribution incurs no penalty. But if the donor doesn't have a qualifying acknowledgment for a single gift of $250 or more by the deadline, the donor can't claim the deduction for that gift.

Do weekly offerings that add up to more than $250 need a written acknowledgment?

Not under the $250 rule. Separate contributions of less than $250 aren't combined, even if a donor's yearly total is $250 or more. Donors still need a bank record or a written communication from the charity for each cash gift.

Can one year-end statement cover several gifts of $250 or more?

Yes. One acknowledgment, such as an annual summary, can cover several gifts. IRS Publication 526 says it should list each contribution and its date and show the total.

How do payroll-deduction gifts work?

Each paycheck deduction is a separate contribution. If $250 or more is withheld from a single paycheck, the donor keeps a pay stub, Form W-2 or other employer document showing the amount, plus a pledge card or other document from the charity stating that it provides no goods or services for payroll-deduction gifts.

Can we email the acknowledgment?

Yes. IRS Publication 1771 says an organization can provide the acknowledgment on paper or electronically, such as by email addressed to the donor.
